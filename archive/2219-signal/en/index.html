How it started

Pulsar-timing stations on four worlds time the same pulsars, all the time. That is why they heard it first.

  1. 2219-09-18 03:12:07 UTC · Farside

    The steadiest clock got louder

    The pulsar-timing station on the far side of the Moon times twelve pulsars around the clock. At 03:12 the steadiest of them, J1909−3744, looked a hundred times brighter, but only in one narrow channel at 1420.55 MHz, just above 1420.405 MHz, the frequency of cold hydrogen. A pulsar shines across the whole radio band, never in one channel. It was filed as local interference.

  2. 2219-09-18 · within half an hour

    Every other station heard it too

    Earth, Isidis on Mars and the Ceres station logged the identical pattern, each at a different moment. Stations compare notes at light speed, so the comparison alone took most of the day.

  3. 2219-09-18

    The delays point at one star

    The arrival times fit a single wave from one direction outside the solar system: RA 11h 47m 44.4s, Dec +00° 48′ 16″. That is Ross 128, a quiet red dwarf in Virgo, much dimmer than the Sun and about twice as old, 10.98 light years away. In 2017 the Arecibo telescope recorded odd signals from this direction; they turned out to be Earth satellites. This time they are not.

  4. 2219-09-21

    It is not natural

    The narrow pulses come at exactly the pulsar's rate. For about seven minutes they sit on its pulses. Then, over about nine minutes, they slide ahead by exactly one pulse and lock on again. Nothing natural copies a pulsar in a single channel, then steps one pulse ahead of it. The beacon comes back every 9 h 41 m, and each pass carries a different transmission in turn, so the duet only returns every fifth pass. The five transmissions.

  5. 2219-09-27

    Only Earth hears it in tune

    At the Moon, Mars and Ceres the two pulse trains sit a fraction of a pulse apart and drift against each other, because the pulsar's rate is Doppler-shifted differently at each place. Corrected to Earth's centre, the gap and the drift both vanish to within nanoseconds. They predicted where Earth would be when the signal arrived, eleven years after they sent it, and tuned the duet for us. Hear it from four places.

  6. 2219-09-28

    The transmitter is on the planet

    The tone sits 0.01% above hydrogen's line because Ross 128 is coming toward us at 31 km/s, and it slides up and down by about 260 kHz every 9.87 days, exactly the time Ross 128 b takes to circle its star. The transmitter rides on that planet, and whoever built it left the tone uncorrected. The pulse timing they did correct, to the nanosecond: the timing is the message. The planet is tidally locked: one side always day, the other always night.

  7. 2219-10-05

    Made public

    Every station published its raw recordings on the same day. This page is part of that announcement.

Decode

Hear the duet from four places

The same pass, as each station recorded it. Only one place hears the two voices in tune.

1 · The pulsar alone

J1909−3744 spins 339.3 times a second. Played as sound, its pulses are a steady buzz a little above middle C.

2 · Their voice joins

A pulse train at exactly the same rate. Where the two are in step they fuse into one louder buzz; half a pulse apart they sound an octave higher.

3 · Pick a station

In the plot, time runs down and one pulsar period runs across. The pulsar's pulse is the middle column. Where theirs sits on it, the two agree.

Distance

Nobody is coming.

10.98 light years

Sun · usRoss 128 b · them

Not us, and not them. Neither side can build a ship that crosses 10.98 light years, and nothing in the signal suggests they are trying. What crosses is light.

So this is contact by correspondence. A question sent today reaches them on 2230. If they answer at once, the reply lands on 2241. Every exchange takes a generation, and the people who start a conversation may not be the ones who finish it.
