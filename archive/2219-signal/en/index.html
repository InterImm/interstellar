Signal

How it started

The MC ledger keeps its clock by timing pulsars, on every world, all the time. That is why it heard first.

  1. 2219-09-18 03:12:07 UTC · Farside

    A residual nobody expected

    The ledger node on the far side of the Moon times twelve pulsars to keep the MC clock. At 03:12 it logged a narrow tone at 1420.55 MHz, just above 1420.405 MHz, the frequency of cold hydrogen, switching on and off. It was filed as local interference.

  2. 2219-09-18 · within half an hour

    Every other ledger heard it too

    Earth, Isidis on Mars and the Ceres node logged the identical pattern, each at a different moment. Ledgers compare notes at light speed, so the comparison alone took most of the day.

  3. 2219-09-18

    The delays point at one star

    The arrival times fit a single wave from one direction outside the solar system: RA 11h 47m 44.4s, Dec +00° 48′ 16″. That is Ross 128, a quiet red dwarf in Virgo, much dimmer than the Sun and about twice as old, 10.98 light years away. In 2017 the Arecibo telescope recorded odd signals from this direction; they turned out to be Earth satellites. This time they are not.

  4. 2219-09-21

    It is not natural

    The pulses are separated by 2, 3, 5, 7, 11 … 47 units: the prime numbers, in order. No pulsar, maser or reflection does that. Then 667 bits follow. The beacon comes back every 9 h 41 m, and each pass carries a different transmission in turn, so the 667 bits only return every fifth pass. The five transmissions.

  5. 2219-09-27

    The 667 bits are a picture

    667 is 23 × 29. Folded into rows of 23, the bits make a drawing. Fold it yourself.

  6. 2219-09-28

    The transmitter is on the planet

    The tone sits 0.01% above hydrogen's line because Ross 128 is coming toward us at 31 km/s, and it slides up and down by about 260 kHz every 9.87 days, exactly the time Ross 128 b takes to circle its star. The transmitter rides on that planet, and whoever built it did not correct for the Doppler shift. The planet is tidally locked: one side always day, the other always night.

  7. 2219-10-05

    Made public

    Every ledger published its raw recordings on the same day. This page is part of that announcement.

Decode

Fold the beacon

You have the same data the ledgers had. Three steps from noise to a picture.

1 · Count the gaps

The preamble is a row of pulses with prime-numbered gaps. Its only job is to say "this is deliberate".

2 · Read the payload

Then 667 bits, ones and zeros, and nothing to say how they fit together.

3 · Fold it

Lay the bits out in rows. Try different widths; only one makes sense.
