Lighthouses in space

A pulsar is a neutron star spinning fast: as heavy as the Sun, as small as a city. Its magnetic poles shine two beams of radio waves that sweep the sky as it turns. Each time a beam crosses Earth, a telescope gets one pulse.

First, untangle this

Two kinds of "frequency"

A pulsar "at 339 hertz" observed "at 1.4 gigahertz": those are two different things.

The spin frequency is the rhythm. However many times a pulsar turns in a second, that many pulses arrive: the slow ones tick once in 8 seconds, the fastest 716 times a second. The number belongs to the star; every telescope measures the same.

The radio frequency is the carrier. Each pulse is a burst of radio noise spread from tens of megahertz to tens of gigahertz, oscillating hundreds of millions to billions of times a second. A telescope takes in one slice of it, its observing band. The band changes what you see: lower frequencies are brighter, but free electrons between the stars delay them more and blur them more.

Ross 128 b's beacon uses the two separately. The carrier is one narrow tone, 1420.55 MHz: hydrogen is the ruler every radio-astronomy civilization knows, the frequency sits in the band pulsar timing watches, so timing stations heard it first, and the uncorrected Doppler shift tells where it comes from. The rhythm is J1909−3744's 339.3 Hz. The tone only carries; the timing is the message. Read the signal archive · Hear the five transmissions

Drag the time window: at nanoseconds the beacon is a clean sine and the pulsar a tangle of noise; at milliseconds both become pulses.

Analysis tools

Pull it out of the noise

Roughly the four steps astronomers take to find and measure a pulsar. Observe for a while, then work on that same data with these tools. For a real try, pick "Mystery signal": nobody knows its period or DM.

Add up the whole band and take a Fourier transform. A periodic signal is a row of evenly spaced spikes in the power spectrum: the fundamental and its harmonics. Narrow pulses have many harmonics, so adding several of them together lifts a weak signal out.

Among tens of thousands of frequencies, noise alone reaches about 10 σ, so a candidate needs 16 σ. A wrong DM smears the pulse and kills the harmonics: a highly dispersed millisecond pulsar only turns up when you search DM after DM.

Free electrons between the stars slow lower frequencies down. The delay goes as one over frequency squared, scaled by the dispersion measure DM: the number of electrons along the line of sight. Dedispersing shifts each channel back by its delay. Get it right and the slant becomes a straight line, the pulse sharpest, the S/N highest.

What is real, what is simulated

There is no live feed. Radio telescopes record enormous amounts of raw data. Observatories put processed data into archives, usually public only after a proprietary period, and nothing online offers a live pulsar signal a web page could plug into. So this page is a simulation, but every number in it comes from real measurements.

Real: periods, dispersion measures, distances and declinations from the ATNF Pulsar Catalogue (rounded). How the flux density changes with frequency comes from measurements in dozens of papers, compiled by the pulsar_spectra project (Swainston et al. 2022) and kept as they are in lib/pulsar-fluxes.json. Telescope gains and system temperatures are typical published values. The noise follows the radiometer equation; the dispersion delay is 4.149 ms × DM ÷ (frequency/GHz)²; scattering grows with DM and falls as frequency to the −3.9, after the empirical fit of Bhat et al. (2004).

Simulated: pulse profiles are schematic Gaussian components, roughly matching measured profiles at 1.4 GHz. Not simulated: period changes from binary orbits, scintillation, pulse-to-pulse variation and radio interference. The "mystery signal" is random.
